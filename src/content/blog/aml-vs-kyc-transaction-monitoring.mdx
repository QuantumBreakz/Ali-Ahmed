**KYC (know your customer)** answers **who** the customer is: identity verification, sanctions screening and a risk rating, mostly at onboarding. **AML (anti-money laundering)** is the **whole programme** a regulated business runs to detect and report money laundering, and **KYC is one part of it**. The rest of AML is about what customers **do** after onboarding: **transaction monitoring**, investigating alerts, filing **suspicious activity reports**, keeping records and periodic re-checks. A customer can pass KYC perfectly and still launder money; transaction monitoring is how you'd notice. Below is a tested Python version of one classic monitoring rule, **structuring detection**, to make the difference concrete.

## KYC vs AML at a glance

| Aspect          | KYC                                          | AML                                                                                  |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Question        | Who is this customer, and how risky?         | Is money being laundered through us, and have we reported it?                        |
| When            | Onboarding, and periodic refreshes           | Continuously, for the whole relationship                                             |
| Main activities | ID verification, screening, risk rating, CDD | KYC plus transaction monitoring, investigations, reporting, training, record keeping |
| Typical output  | Approve, reject or review a customer         | Alerts, cases, suspicious activity reports                                           |

**CDD** (customer due diligence) is the KYC work itself, deepened to **EDD** (enhanced due diligence) for higher-risk customers. **KYB** applies the same idea to businesses and the people who own them; see [KYB vs KYC and beneficial ownership](/blog/kyb-vs-kyc-beneficial-ownership/). The longer overview is in [KYC vs AML vs KYB vs CDD](/blog/kyc-vs-aml-vs-kyb/).

## A monitoring rule: structuring

Many countries require banks to report cash transactions above a threshold; in the US, cash transactions over **$10,000** trigger a Currency Transaction Report. **Structuring** is splitting deposits to stay under that line, and in the US it is itself an offence, even when the money is legitimate. A rule to catch it:

```python title="structuring.py"
from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

@dataclass
class Deposit:
    customer: str
    amount: float
    at: datetime
    cash: bool = True

def structuring_alerts(deposits: list[Deposit], threshold: float = 10_000, window: timedelta = timedelta(days=7),
                       near: float = 0.8, min_count: int = 3) -> list[dict]:
    """Flag customers who make several cash deposits just under the reporting threshold within a rolling window,
    adding up to more than the threshold. Produces alerts for review, not verdicts."""
    alerts = []
    by_customer: dict[str, list[Deposit]] = {}
    for d in sorted(deposits, key=lambda d: d.at):
        if d.cash and near * threshold <= d.amount < threshold:
            by_customer.setdefault(d.customer, []).append(d)
    for customer, ds in by_customer.items():
        start = 0
        for end in range(len(ds)):
            while ds[end].at - ds[start].at > window:
                start += 1
            group = ds[start:end + 1]
            if len(group) >= min_count and sum(d.amount for d in group) > threshold:
                alerts.append({"customer": customer, "count": len(group), "total": sum(d.amount for d in group),
                               "from": group[0].at.date().isoformat(), "to": group[-1].at.date().isoformat()})
                break  # one alert per customer per run; the reviewer sees the full history
    return alerts
```

The test feeds five customers through it:

| Customer | Pattern                                              | Alert?                       |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| A        | $9,500, $9,800, $9,200 cash within 5 days            | **Yes**: 3 deposits, $28,500 |
| B        | The same amounts, spread over 20 days                | No                           |
| C        | $12,000 and $15,000: above the line, reported anyway | No                           |
| D        | Three $9,900 **wires**, not cash                     | No                           |
| E        | Small cash deposits of $2,000–3,000                  | No                           |

## Why rules alone aren't enough

The parameters (80% of the threshold, 7 days, 3 deposits) are guesses until tuned on real alerts. Set them loose and analysts drown in false positives; set them tight and patient structurers spread deposits over a longer window, as customer B did. Production systems run many rules plus models, score alerts by risk, and **record why every alert was closed**, the same audit principle as in [sanctions name screening](/blog/sanctions-name-screening-fuzzy-matching/).

KYC data makes monitoring better: the risk rating and expected activity captured at onboarding ("salary account, about $3,000 a month") are what makes $28,500 in cash unusual. That's the practical link between the two. Where AI helps, it's in prioritising and summarising alerts for analysts, not in making the reporting decision.

## Summary

- **KYC** = who the customer is, at onboarding and periodic refresh. **AML** = the whole programme, of which KYC is one part.
- AML adds **transaction monitoring**, investigation, **reporting** and record keeping.
- **Structuring** (splitting cash deposits under a reporting threshold) is a classic monitoring rule; the tested version flags 3+ near-threshold cash deposits within 7 days.
- Tune rules on real alerts, record closure reasons, and feed KYC risk data into monitoring.
