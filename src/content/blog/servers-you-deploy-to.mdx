On a modern platform your application isn't attached to a particular server. You deploy a **workload** and declare a **desired state** ("keep three copies of this running"), and an orchestrator decides which machine runs it, moves it when a machine fails, and starts more copies when traffic grows. Servers still exist. They just stopped being the thing you deploy to.

_Part 2 of the Modern Deployments series. Part 1: [how free tiers work](/blog/how-free-tiers-work/)._

## Is "my app runs on an EC2 instance" wrong?

I once heard someone say, "My app is running on an EC2 instance." It's not wrong. It's just not the whole story anymore.

Take a typical deployment on Vercel, Render or Railway. You push code to GitHub. A build pipeline packages the application, often into a container image. The image goes to a registry. The platform schedules it somewhere in its fleet. At no point do you pick a machine, SSH into it, or even learn where your app ended up. That's intentional.

## Why did containers change deployment?

Unlike a virtual machine, a container doesn't carry its own operating system. Containers on a host **share the host's Linux kernel** and keep their own isolated processes, network and filesystem (through kernel features called namespaces and cgroups).

That makes them light: they start in seconds or less, and many fit on one machine. A platform can move, restart or scale your app quickly because there is so little to boot.

## What happens when the machine fails?

Ten years ago, a dead host probably meant downtime and a pager going off.

Today an orchestrator like **Kubernetes** notices that the actual state (two copies running) no longer matches the desired state (three copies) and **schedules a replacement on a healthy node**. Managed platforms run a similar control loop. You just never see it.

That loop is the important idea:

1. You declare what should be true.
2. The control plane keeps watching what _is_ true.
3. When they differ, it acts until they match again.

## What does this change for developers?

- **Treat instances as disposable.** Local disk and in-memory state can vanish when your app is moved. Put state in a database, object store or cache.
- **Health checks matter.** The orchestrator decides what is "healthy" from your health endpoint, so make it honest.
- **Scale is configuration, not hardware.** Replicas and autoscaling rules replace buying a bigger box.
- **Which CPU runs your code is an implementation detail**, and it can change during the day.

This is also why [cold starts](/blog/serverless-cold-starts-explained/) exist: if the platform can place your workload anywhere, sometimes it has to create that place first.
