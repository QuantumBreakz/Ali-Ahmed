Face verification turns two face images into **embeddings** (vectors) and compares them. If their similarity is above a **threshold**, they're declared the same person. Every threshold makes two kinds of mistake: a **false match** (an impostor accepted, measured as **FMR**, also called FAR) and a **false non-match** (the real person rejected, **FNMR**, also called FRR). Raising the threshold lowers one and raises the other. The right threshold isn't a property of the model; it's a business decision, made from data, about which mistake costs more.

## How does face verification work?

1. **Detect and align** the face in each image (the selfie and the document portrait), so eyes and mouth sit in standard positions.
2. **Embed** each face with a recognition model. Similar faces map to nearby vectors.
3. **Compare**, usually with cosine similarity.
4. **Decide** with a threshold.

This is **1:1 verification** ("is this the claimed person?"). It's different from 1:N identification ("who is this, out of a million people?"), where errors scale with the size of the database. KYC is mostly 1:1, as one stage of the [verification pipeline](/blog/how-kyc-identity-verification-works/).

## What are FMR and FNMR?

To measure them, you need labelled pairs:

- **Genuine pairs:** two images of the same person. FNMR is the share of these scoring **below** the threshold.
- **Impostor pairs:** two images of different people. FMR is the share of these scoring **at or above** the threshold.

```python title="Error rates at a threshold"
import numpy as np

def error_rates(genuine: np.ndarray, impostor: np.ndarray, threshold: float):
    fnmr = float(np.mean(genuine < threshold))
    fmr = float(np.mean(impostor >= threshold))
    return fmr, fnmr

def threshold_for_fmr(impostor: np.ndarray, target_fmr: float) -> float:
    return float(np.quantile(impostor, 1 - target_fmr))
```

`threshold_for_fmr` picks the threshold that lets through the target share of impostors. You then read FNMR at that threshold, and that tells you how many real customers will fail.

## How do you choose the threshold?

Start from the mistake that's more expensive, and fix its rate:

- **Fix FMR first** when a false accept is costly: bank accounts, payments, account recovery. Typical targets are 1 in 10,000 down to 1 in 1,000,000 impostor attempts. Then check that FNMR is acceptable.
- Plot **FNMR against FMR** across thresholds (a DET curve) to see the trade-off. A better model moves the whole curve down, which is the only way to improve both at once.
- **Send the uncertain middle to a human** instead of forcing a yes or no. A band just under the threshold, reviewed manually, often costs less than either kind of error.

Be careful with small numbers. Estimating an FMR of one in a million needs **millions of impostor pairs**; with 10,000 pairs, "zero false matches" says very little.

## What moves the numbers in real KYC?

Benchmarks use clean photos. Real verification compares a **phone selfie** with a **small, printed, sometimes years-old document portrait**, and the error rates move with:

- **Image quality:** glare on the card, low light, motion blur, heavy compression.
- **Ageing:** a passport photo can be ten years old. The gap between genuine scores and impostor scores shrinks as the age difference grows.
- **Demographics:** NIST's evaluations (NIST IR 8280, 2019) found error rates for many algorithms varied by age, sex and race, with false match rates often differing by large factors between groups. **Measure FMR and FNMR per group** on your own data before choosing one global threshold.
- **Attacks:** a high similarity score from a printed photo is still a "match". That's why [liveness detection](/blog/face-liveness-detection-explained/) runs alongside face matching.

## The checklist

- Pick the threshold **from your own genuine and impostor pairs**, not a vendor default.
- **Fix the costly error rate first**, then measure the other.
- Report rates **with sample sizes**, and per demographic group.
- Keep a **manual review band** for borderline scores.
- **Log scores, not just decisions**, so you can re-tune later without re-running every verification.
